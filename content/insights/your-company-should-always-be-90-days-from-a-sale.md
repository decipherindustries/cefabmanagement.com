A buyer calls and wants a data room in 30 days. Most founders discover they're twelve months from ready, not twelve weeks.

Exit readiness tends to be treated as a project that starts when someone makes an offer. Then the normalising, the documenting and the management layer all happen at once, under time pressure, while the business still needs running. Every gap patched in a hurry becomes a negotiation point for the buyer.

I'd rather treat it as a standard you keep every month:

1. Financials a buyer can audit. Revenue recognised properly, a clear entity structure, a normalised EBITDA bridge kept current, not reconstructed for diligence.
2. A management layer that runs without the founder. If decisions collapse back to one person during diligence, it shows up in price or structure.
3. A data room that's always live. Financials and KPIs refreshed monthly, contracts and HR quarterly, and an update whenever something material happens. A couple of hours a quarter, and much of it can be automated.
4. A two-page summary tied to real KPIs that you could send to a serious buyer today.

None of this is just about the exit. The work that makes a company sellable is the same work that makes it easy to run and gives you the right insights. Revenue recognised properly and a current EBITDA bridge mean a faster monthly close and numbers the management team and shareholders trust. A live data room means the board pack is mostly written before the month ends.

I once spoke about this with the founder of a group of industrial companies he has built over more than thirty years. He told me he has an M&A specialist run a *full due diligence audit* on every one of them once a year, whether or not a sale is planned. His reasoning: "It forces each company to know which numbers a buyer will look at, and to have the story behind them ready. If a good offer comes, we can sell at the right price without first spending a year getting ready." That offer came in 2022, when he sold one of his businesses for about €1 billion.

The difference between a clean exit and a painful one is rarely the deal itself. It's whether the business was ready before anyone asked.
